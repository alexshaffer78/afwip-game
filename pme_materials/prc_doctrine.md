# PRC and PLA Doctrine Context for Air Force Wargame Indo-Pacific

## Purpose

This document provides a detailed overview of People's Republic of China (PRC) and People's Liberation Army (PLA) strategic and operational thought for use as context in a large language model (LLM). It is designed to accompany:

1. a separate file containing the rules of **Air Force Wargame Indo-Pacific**;
2. a text description of the current game state; and
3. a question about how a possible PRC play relates to Chinese military concepts, organizational logic, likely campaign priorities, or operational tradeoffs.

The file focuses on aerospace and joint operations: the PLA Air Force (PLAAF), PLA Rocket Force (PLARF), PLA Navy aviation, Aerospace Force, Cyberspace Force, Information Support Force, Joint Logistics Support Force, and their relationships to theater command campaigns.

This document does **not** create game rules, predict a real-world PRC decision, or claim access to classified plans. It is a structured synthesis of open-source scholarship and translations published by the **China Aerospace Studies Institute (CASI)** at Air University.

All substantive sourcing comes exclusively from CASI and CASI-hosted publications at `https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/CASI/`.

# Critical Source and Interpretation Warning

## CASI is not the PRC's doctrine publisher

CASI is a United States Department of the Air Force research institute. Its mission is to improve understanding of the strategy, doctrine, operating concepts, capabilities, organization, personnel, training, infrastructure, and limitations of China's aerospace forces.[SRC-01]

CASI sources used here fall into several categories:

- **CASI analytical studies:** assessments written by CASI personnel or CASI-sponsored researchers.
- **CASI primers and organizational references:** syntheses of current open-source evidence.
- **“In Their Own Words” translations:** Chinese military texts translated and hosted by CASI.
- **Short-form CASI articles:** updates on organization, training, equipment, exercises, and reforms.

A CASI-hosted translation reveals how a Chinese military text frames a problem. It does not automatically establish that every PLA unit follows the concept, that the concept is current, or that the capability has been demonstrated.

## “Doctrine” is used broadly in this document

Unlike the United States Air Force's publicly accessible doctrine hierarchy, the PLA does not provide outside readers with one complete and current set of authoritative publications. This file therefore uses **PRC/PLA doctrine** as shorthand for a structured body of:

- strategic guidance;
- military theory;
- campaign concepts;
- official organizational arrangements;
- professional military education texts;
- training and exercise evidence;
- CASI assessments of demonstrated strengths and limitations.

The LLM should never collapse these categories into a single level of certainty.

## Required evidence labels

When using this file, distinguish:

- **PLA-stated:** a concept expressed in a CASI-hosted Chinese military text.
- **CASI-assessed:** an analytical judgment made in a CASI study.
- **Observed:** an organization, activity, or capability documented in open sources and reported by CASI.
- **Aspirational:** a concept the PLA discusses or seeks but may not yet execute reliably.
- **Inferred:** an analytical connection between the doctrine and the game state.

## Translation caution

CASI's translation of the 2020 *Science of Military Strategy* provides unusually broad access to PLA strategic thought, but CASI notes that it was produced with an automated translation system and may contain inconsistencies.[SRC-03] Use its terminology for conceptual orientation, not as a perfect official English rendering.

# Instructions to the LLM

## Keep five information layers separate

Clearly separate:

1. **Game rules** — binding mechanics determining legality and resolution.
2. **Board-state facts** — pieces, positions, damage, cards, missions, timing, resources, and known threats.
3. **PLA-stated concepts** — ideas found in translated Chinese military writings.
4. **CASI assessments** — external judgments about capability, organization, progress, and limitations.
5. **Your inference** — how the concepts may illuminate a particular play.

Never treat doctrine as an extra rules engine. Never state that the PRC “would” choose an action when the evidence supports only that it “might,” “could,” or “would have doctrinal reasons to consider” it.

## Do not mirror-image U.S. doctrine

Do not assume that a PRC player should organize objectives, command relationships, timing, escalation, or air operations exactly as a U.S. air component would.

Important differences include:

- the PLA is the armed wing of the Chinese Communist Party, not a politically neutral national institution;
- political objectives, regime security, sovereignty claims, deterrence, and information effects are integrated with military operations;
- theater commands are joint warfighting organizations, while services and strategic arms have important force-development and support roles;
- missile, information, space, cyber, and electromagnetic effects may be central to an air campaign rather than merely supporting it;
- PLA writings often emphasize destroying or paralyzing an opposing **operational system**, not simply defeating equivalent platforms;
- “active defense” can include offensive action, initiative, preemption under certain conditions, and counterattack while remaining strategically framed as defensive;
- the PLA may seek to control the scale, intensity, pace, geography, and duration of confrontation rather than treating escalation as an accidental byproduct.

## Use a system model

When analyzing a board state, map each side as an operational system:

- leadership and command;
- intelligence and early warning;
- communications and data;
- space support;
- cyber and electromagnetic capabilities;
- air and missile forces;
- bases and launch positions;
- logistics;
- mobility;
- protection;
- repair and reconstitution;
- alliances and access;
- information and political conditions.

Then ask which nodes, links, and functions are:

- critical;
- vulnerable;
- redundant;
- recoverable;
- difficult to replace;
- time sensitive;
- necessary for the opponent's main operation.

## Cite this document specifically

Doctrine advice is only useful if it is traceable. Whenever you use a concept, **cite it specifically**: name the section by its number and heading and quote the exact passage. This document is numbered (`## 13. System destruction warfare`) and tags its sources `[SRC-NN]`, so a citation looks like:

> *§13 "System destruction warfare": "disrupt, paralyze, or destroy the critical functions of the opponent's operational system while preserving one's own."*

Quote **verbatim** from this document (a few words up to one sentence). **Never invent** section numbers, headings, quotations, or `[SRC-NN]` tags — if you cannot find supporting text, reason qualitatively and say so rather than manufacturing a citation. Keep the evidence labels (PLA-stated / CASI-assessed / Observed / Aspirational / Inferred) in mind when you quote: a translated PLA concept is not the same as a demonstrated capability.

## Recommended response structure

1. **Rules legality**
2. **PRC political-military objective**
3. **Operational-system diagnosis**
4. **Proposed sequence of actions**
5. **Joint integration requirements**
6. **Opponent's most likely response**
7. **Opponent's most dangerous response**
8. **Escalation and control considerations**
9. **Sustainment and follow-through**
10. **Assessment and confidence**

## Confidence language

Use:

- **High confidence:** supported by current CASI organizational reporting and explicit game facts.
- **Moderate confidence:** supported by recurring concepts but dependent on uncertain implementation.
- **Low confidence:** relies on aspirational writing, ambiguous translation, incomplete board data, or speculative adversary intent.

# Part I — Political and Strategic Foundations

## 1. The PLA as the Party's armed force

A foundational distinction is that the PLA is the armed force of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). CASI reporting on current reforms repeatedly emphasizes the principle of the Party's absolute leadership over the military.[SRC-07]

This affects doctrine and command in several ways:

- military operations serve political objectives defined by Party leadership;
- political reliability is treated as a component of combat effectiveness;
- strategic decisions are centralized under the Central Military Commission (CMC);
- political work, discipline, information, and organizational control are integrated into military readiness;
- military success is judged partly by whether it creates a favorable political outcome.

For wargame analysis, do not assume that maximizing destroyed units is the PRC objective. A move may instead aim to:

- alter an opponent's political calculation;
- demonstrate resolve;
- impose a fait accompli;
- divide a coalition;
- create bargaining leverage;
- normalize a new operating pattern;
- deny outside intervention;
- preserve a claim of defensive legitimacy;
- control escalation while improving the PRC position.

## 2. War as a political and systemic contest

CASI's *Spectrum of Conflict* study notes that Chinese military writing emphasizes the political character of warfare and the use of military and nonmilitary instruments across competition, crisis, and conflict.[SRC-04]

PLA strategic thought does not require a clean boundary between:

- peacetime preparation;
- coercive military signaling;
- law-enforcement activity;
- information activity;
- mobilization;
- crisis response;
- limited use of force;
- large-scale war.

Activities may serve several purposes simultaneously. A patrol can collect intelligence, test reactions, signal resolve, train forces, establish presence, and change what becomes normal.

### 2.1 Implication for the game

A move can have value even without immediate combat if it:

- improves posture;
- reveals an opponent's response pattern;
- establishes access;
- creates ambiguity;
- prepares a later attack;
- strains enemy readiness;
- complicates alliance decisions;
- changes the perceived threshold for action.

## 3. Active defense

The translated 2020 *Science of Military Strategy* presents **active defense** as the fundamental strategic idea connecting overall strategic defensiveness with active and offensive action.[SRC-03]

At the strategic level, Chinese military texts frame the PRC as defensive. At campaign and tactical levels, however, active defense can include:

- seizing initiative;
- anticipatory preparation;
- offensive counteraction;
- preemptive action when an attack is judged imminent;
- rapid counterattack;
- attacking operational vulnerabilities;
- transferring from defense to offense;
- using force to stop or limit conflict.

### 3.1 Active defense is not passive defense

A weak interpretation is:

> “The PLA waits to absorb the first blow.”

A stronger interpretation is:

> “The PLA seeks political justification for a strategically defensive posture while retaining operational freedom to act first, seize initiative, and conduct offensive campaigns when leaders judge that core interests are threatened or conflict is unavoidable.”

### 3.2 Wargame questions

- What event could the PRC portray as crossing a threshold?
- Which action seizes initiative while preserving a defensive narrative?
- Is a counterattack intended to restore the status quo or create a new one?
- Does the move prevent the opposing force from completing its deployment?
- Does it create a quick operational advantage before a larger coalition can respond?

## 4. Strategic initiative

PLA writings place strong value on controlling initiative. Initiative is more than moving first. It means shaping the sequence of decisions so the opponent must react under unfavorable conditions.

Initiative can be produced through:

- superior information;
- deception;
- concealed mobilization;
- rapid concentration of firepower;
- control of key time windows;
- attacks on command and information systems;
- simultaneous threats in several domains;
- unexpected direction, timing, or method;
- forcing the adversary to protect multiple critical assets.

A force can fire first yet lose initiative if the attack does not change the opponent's choices. Conversely, a force can gain initiative through posture and coercion without firing.

## 5. The spectrum of conflict

CASI assesses that there is no single publicly available, official PLA spectrum or escalation ladder. Chinese sources use overlapping models and deliberately ambiguous thresholds.[SRC-04]

CASI also notes that Chinese authors may reject the Western label **gray zone** while still embracing:

- strategic ambiguity;
- incremental pressure;
- escalation management;
- coercion below a war threshold;
- integration of military and nonmilitary instruments;
- peacetime actions that improve wartime position.

### 5.1 Analytical consequence

Do not force every board action into a binary category of peace or war. Ask:

- What level of force is being used?
- What political message accompanies it?
- Is the action reversible?
- Is attribution clear?
- Does it change facts on the ground, sea, air, or information environment?
- What response would be disproportionate and therefore difficult for the opponent?
- Does it preserve a route to de-escalation?

## 6. Effective control

CASI's study of **effective control** describes a recurring PLA concern with controlling competition, crisis, and war.[SRC-05] The concept encompasses:

- building a favorable posture in peacetime;
- preventing or controlling crises;
- seizing opportunities during changing conditions;
- controlling the initiation, scale, intensity, pace, geographic scope, and termination of conflict;
- achieving objectives quickly and at acceptable cost;
- preventing an advantageous military action from producing an unfavorable political outcome.

Effective control should not be understood as proof that escalation is easily controllable. CASI assesses that the theory is evolving and that some prescriptions—especially “seizing opportunities”—could themselves be escalatory.

### 6.1 Wargame implications

A PRC player may prefer a move that:

- creates a bounded operational result;
- offers an off-ramp after a favorable change;
- limits geographic spread;
- prevents outside forces from entering;
- controls the tempo before the opponent mobilizes;
- preserves high-end capabilities as a deterrent;
- avoids unnecessary attacks on politically sensitive targets.

## 7. Deterrence and compellence

The translated *Science of Military Strategy* treats strategic deterrence as the integrated use of military and nonmilitary power, resolve, and communication to influence an opponent's decisions.[SRC-03]

Its logic includes both:

- **deterrence:** preventing an action; and
- **compellence:** causing an opponent to stop, change, or undertake an action.

Three recurring elements are:

1. **Capability or strength**
2. **Resolve or determination**
3. **Transmission of information**

A capability that is invisible or unbelievable may not deter. A signal that is not backed by usable force may be dismissed. A threat that leaves no acceptable exit may provoke resistance rather than compliance.

### 7.1 Integrated strategic deterrence

PLA writings frequently consider military power alongside:

- diplomatic pressure;
- economic leverage;
- legal claims;
- public messaging;
- technology;
- mobilization;
- demonstrations and exercises;
- conventional and nuclear forces.

For game use, limit analysis to instruments represented in the rules, but recognize that a military move may be designed to communicate rather than immediately destroy.

## 8. War control and rapid decision

PLA texts often emphasize achieving objectives quickly and at limited cost. This preference is linked to:

- avoiding prolonged exposure to superior coalition resources;
- preventing mobilization and reinforcement;
- limiting economic disruption;
- reducing political uncertainty;
- creating a new situation before the opponent can coordinate.

This does not mean every PLA plan assumes a short war will succeed. It means front-loaded operations, rapid system disruption, and early political leverage may be especially attractive.

## 9. People's war and national mobilization

The translated *Science of Military Strategy* continues to connect modern warfare with **people's war**, adapted to contemporary technology and national power.[SRC-03]

In modern application, this can include:

- mobilization of civilian transportation;
- commercial communications and data;
- scientific and industrial support;
- infrastructure;
- local governments;
- militia and reserve forces;
- national defense mobilization;
- civilian technical specialists;
- economic and political instruments.

Do not interpret people's war only as mass infantry. In an aerospace campaign, its relevance may include the civilian and commercial foundation supporting:

- satellite production and launch;
- airfields and transport;
- shipyards and ports;
- telecommunications;
- electronics;
- repair;
- logistics;
- research and development.

## 10. Local wars under informationized conditions

A major strand of PLA modernization has been preparation for **local wars under informationized conditions**. The phrase captures several ideas:

- conflict may be geographically limited yet technologically intense;
- information networks connect dispersed forces;
- precision weapons allow attacks on critical nodes;
- joint operations replace isolated service action;
- command speed and information quality influence combat power;
- space, cyber, and electromagnetic operations are integral to physical combat.

“Local” does not mean tactically small. It can describe a high-intensity regional war with major strategic consequences.

## 11. From mechanization to informationization to intelligentization

CASI's 2026 study of PRC research on kill processes describes a conceptual progression:[SRC-08]

- **Mechanized warfare:** platform-centric forces and relatively linear kill chains.
- **Informationized warfare:** networked forces, kill webs, data-enabled integration, and system confrontation.
- **Intelligentized warfare:** greater use of artificial intelligence, autonomy, human-machine teaming, adaptive networks, and kill meshes.

This progression is neither complete nor uniform. Legacy platforms, human decision processes, and organizational barriers continue alongside advanced experimentation.

### 11.1 Do not equate discussion with capability

CASI explicitly cautions that PRC research on kill webs and meshes shows conceptual interest and development activity, but available evidence does not establish that the PLA has operationalized a mature, adaptive kill web at scale.[SRC-08]

# Part II — Operational Theory

## 12. Systems confrontation

PLA operational thought often treats modern war as a confrontation between opposing **systems of systems** rather than as a collection of independent platform duels.[SRC-03][SRC-08]

An operational system can include:

- reconnaissance;
- command and control;
- communications;
- data processing;
- space support;
- navigation and timing;
- electronic warfare;
- firepower;
- maneuver;
- protection;
- sustainment;
- repair;
- political and information support.

The system's combat effectiveness depends on the relationships among its parts.

### 12.1 System effectiveness is not the sum of platforms

Ten advanced aircraft may produce little operational effect if they lack:

- target data;
- protected bases;
- weapons;
- tanker support;
- communications;
- command authority;
- maintenance;
- airspace coordination.

Conversely, less capable platforms may create substantial effects when integrated into a resilient reconnaissance-strike network.

## 13. System destruction warfare

A central concept is to disrupt, paralyze, or destroy the critical functions of the opponent's operational system while preserving one's own.[SRC-03][SRC-08]

This can be achieved by attacking:

- key nodes;
- high-value links;
- information flow;
- command relationships;
- sensors;
- communications relays;
- bases;
- logistics;
- decision processes;
- critical enabling platforms;
- system recovery mechanisms.

The objective may be **functional paralysis**, not complete physical destruction.

### 13.1 Node-link-function analysis

For each possible target, ask:

- **Node:** What entity is it?
- **Link:** What connections depend on it?
- **Function:** What operational task does it enable?
- **Redundancy:** What substitutes exist?
- **Recovery:** How quickly can the function return?
- **Timing:** When is it most valuable or vulnerable?
- **Exploitation:** What friendly action becomes possible if it fails?

### 13.2 Game implication

Attacking the strongest enemy combat token may be less valuable than disabling the sensor, tanker, headquarters, base, or logistics node that makes several tokens effective.

## 14. Information-led operations

CASI's study of the PLA view of the information domain describes information systems as the connective tissue that binds units and weapons into an operational whole.[SRC-06]

The translated *Science of Military Strategy* describes an approach that can be summarized as **information-led and system-breaking**:[SRC-03]

- gain and preserve information advantage;
- protect the friendly operational system;
- identify the structure and vulnerabilities of the opposing system;
- control or disrupt information flow;
- apply multi-domain effects to critical nodes;
- exploit resulting paralysis with physical operations.

### 14.1 Information as a functional domain

PLA writing may treat the information domain as comparable in operational importance to physical domains. It includes or connects:

- network information;
- electromagnetic activity;
- data;
- cognition;
- command information;
- intelligence;
- space-enabled services;
- cyber activity.

Terminology varies across sources and reforms. Avoid imposing a rigid Western domain taxonomy on every Chinese concept.

## 15. Campaign operational strengths

CASI's information-domain study describes a PLA framework in which campaign effectiveness includes interacting forms of strength such as:[SRC-06]

- **information power;**
- **firepower;**
- **maneuver power;**
- **assault power;**
- **protection power.**

These should not be analyzed independently.

For example:

- information power finds and characterizes targets;
- firepower creates damage or disruption;
- maneuver places forces in advantageous position;
- assault power exploits openings;
- protection preserves the system long enough to continue.

## 16. Multi-domain integrated joint operations

The translated *Science of Military Strategy* identifies multi-domain integrated joint operations as a basic form of modern PLA operations.[SRC-03]

The concept emphasizes:

- unified command;
- a network information system;
- integration across services and strategic arms;
- coordination of physical and information effects;
- operation around an overall strategic intent;
- maximizing system-level effect rather than service-level output.

### 16.1 Jointness is an operational requirement and a challenge

PLA reform has created joint theater commands, but organization does not guarantee seamless joint execution. CASI studies repeatedly distinguish organizational aspiration from demonstrated proficiency.

An LLM should therefore analyze two questions:

1. What would a doctrinally integrated PLA campaign attempt?
2. What coordination frictions or dependencies could prevent it?

## 17. Multidomain precision warfare and kill processes

CASI's kill-process study finds that PRC analysts increasingly discuss:

- kill chains;
- kill webs;
- kill meshes;
- multidomain precision warfare;
- cross-domain sensing and engagement;
- dynamic task organization;
- human-machine integration;
- resilient and reconfigurable networks.[SRC-08]

A **kill chain** is relatively linear. A **kill web** provides multiple pathways among sensors, decision nodes, and effectors. A **kill mesh** is an aspirational, more adaptive and intelligent network.

### 17.1 Desired characteristics

PRC research seeks to:

- shorten detection-to-engagement timelines;
- connect more sensors and shooters;
- identify high-value nodes;
- automate portions of planning and evaluation;
- improve interoperability;
- enable crewed-uncrewed teaming;
- make the network resilient to node loss;
- generate alternate engagement paths.

### 17.2 Current limitations

CASI cautions that:

- many concepts remain research or experimentation;
- human experience still drives important decisions;
- some methods are better at after-action evaluation than real-time generation;
- interoperability and data quality remain challenges;
- an operationally mature kill web has not been conclusively demonstrated.

## 18. Integrated firepower

PLA operations may combine:

- ballistic and cruise missiles;
- aircraft;
- long-range rockets;
- naval fires;
- electronic attack;
- cyber effects;
- unmanned systems;
- special operations;
- information activity.

The purpose is not necessarily simultaneous use of every capability. It is coordinated effect against the opposing system.

### 18.1 Firepower concentration

PLA theory often favors concentration of effects against key operational points while forces may remain physically distributed. This supports:

- surprise;
- saturation;
- overwhelming local defense;
- rapid creation of a campaign opening;
- reduction of high-value enemy capabilities before they can disperse.

### 18.2 Firepower must be followed by exploitation

A successful opening strike that is not connected to maneuver, blockade, air-control, landing, or coercive objectives may expend scarce munitions without deciding the campaign.

## 19. Sequencing and operational windows

A system-destruction approach often depends on sequence:

1. understand the opponent's system;
2. conceal intentions and prepare forces;
3. create information and electromagnetic advantage;
4. suppress warning and command;
5. attack critical nodes;
6. create a local operational window;
7. exploit with air, maritime, missile, or landing forces;
8. assess, re-strike, and control escalation.

This sequence is a synthesis, not a universal fixed PLA checklist. It is useful for examining dependencies.

## 20. Surprise, deception, and stratagem

PLA military thought places enduring value on:

- concealment;
- deception;
- attacking weakness;
- creating false impressions;
- changing tempo;
- using unexpected combinations;
- separating political signaling from operational preparation;
- inducing the opponent to reveal or misallocate forces.

In game analysis, deception is useful only if the rules represent hidden information, reactions, cards, deployments, or opportunity costs. Do not invent a deception effect outside the rules.

## 21. Economy of force through system attack

System destruction seeks efficiency by attacking critical functions rather than every combat unit. It may create favorable exchange ratios by:

- disabling several units through one enabling node;
- forcing costly defensive dispersal;
- reducing sortie generation;
- creating false tracks or communications overload;
- attacking repair or resupply capacity;
- making forces irrelevant by denying access or targeting.

## 22. Operational control of time

Time is a central operational variable:

- warning time;
- decision time;
- missile flight time;
- sortie generation time;
- repair time;
- reinforcement time;
- mobilization time;
- coalition consultation time;
- target exposure time.

A PLA-style campaign may seek to compress friendly decision and strike cycles while expanding those of the opponent.

## 23. Control of escalation

PLA writings discuss controlling:

- the threshold of violence;
- target categories;
- geography;
- intensity;
- duration;
- weapons used;
- political signaling;
- pause and termination conditions.

This is a goal, not a guarantee. Complex, high-tempo, multi-domain attacks can produce misperception and uncontrolled responses.

### 23.1 Wargame questions

- Which targets are militarily useful but politically escalatory?
- Does the action threaten strategic command or nuclear forces?
- Can the opponent distinguish conventional from nuclear intent?
- Does the move create an off-ramp?
- What signal accompanies restraint?
- What if the opponent interprets limited action as preparation for a larger attack?

# Part III — Command, Organization, and Force Relationships

## 24. Current high-level organization

CASI's 2026 *PLA Aerospace Power Primer* describes a PLA organized under the CMC with:[SRC-02]

### Four services

1. PLA Army
2. PLA Navy
3. PLA Air Force
4. PLA Rocket Force

### Four strategic arms

1. Aerospace Force
2. Cyberspace Force
3. Information Support Force
4. Joint Logistics Support Force

The former Strategic Support Force was dissolved in the 2024 reform. Its principal functions were redistributed among new or restructured organizations.[SRC-02][SRC-07]

## 25. Central Military Commission

The CMC is the highest military command body under Party leadership. It exercises strategic direction and retains control over major political and military decisions.

For game analysis, centralization implies:

- strategic objectives and escalation decisions may be tightly controlled;
- theater commanders execute within political boundaries;
- high-consequence actions may require higher approval;
- a disruption of local command does not necessarily remove national control;
- centralized decisionmaking can support unity but may slow adaptation or discourage initiative.

## 26. Theater commands

The PLA's five theater commands are joint operational commands oriented toward strategic directions:

- Eastern Theater Command
- Southern Theater Command
- Western Theater Command
- Northern Theater Command
- Central Theater Command

For an Indo-Pacific contingency, the Eastern and Southern Theater Commands are especially relevant, depending on geography and scenario.

Theater commands are designed to:

- plan joint operations;
- command forces assigned or allocated for campaigns;
- integrate service and strategic-arm capabilities;
- coordinate operations in a strategic direction.

Services retain major responsibilities for force construction, training, administration, and provision of capabilities.

## 27. Command tension: centralized control and battlefield speed

PLA modernization seeks both:

- centralized and unified command; and
- faster, distributed, information-enabled operations.

These aims can conflict.

Potential friction includes:

- approval bottlenecks;
- reluctance to deviate from a plan;
- political supervision;
- limited trust in subordinate initiative;
- communications dependence;
- cross-service data and procedure differences;
- difficulty adapting scripted operations to unexpected resistance.

Do not assume this tension makes the PLA incapable of adaptation. Treat it as a variable that can become more important under degraded communications and fast-changing conditions.

## 28. PLA Air Force

CASI's current primer describes the PLAAF as a strategic service developing broader reach, integrated air and missile defense, offensive strike, air mobility, airborne, early warning, reconnaissance, and unmanned capabilities.[SRC-02]

Major functional categories include:

- fighter aviation;
- bomber aviation;
- special-mission aircraft;
- transport aviation;
- unmanned aviation;
- airborne forces;
- surface-to-air missile forces;
- radar and early-warning forces;
- airfield and support organizations.

### 28.1 PLAAF operational purposes

The PLAAF may contribute to:

- air defense;
- local or theater air superiority;
- long-range precision strike;
- strategic signaling;
- support to maritime and land campaigns;
- transport and airborne operations;
- reconnaissance and early warning;
- defense of key political, industrial, and military areas;
- support to anti-intervention operations.

### 28.2 “Strategic air force”

The shift toward a “strategic air force” reflects an aspiration to operate beyond territorial air defense and influence regional or strategic outcomes. This includes longer-range platforms, aerial refueling, bombers, transports, special-mission aircraft, and improved command networks.

Strategic reach remains constrained by:

- tanker capacity;
- overseas access;
- basing;
- maintenance;
- crew proficiency;
- protection of large support aircraft;
- joint integration;
- contested information systems.

## 29. PLA Navy aviation

PLAN aviation includes carrier aviation, shipborne aviation, and other naval air capabilities. CASI reporting on a 2023 reorganization indicates that much shore-based PLAN fighter, bomber, radar, air-defense, and airfield infrastructure was transferred to the PLAAF, allowing PLAN aviation to focus more heavily on carrier and shipborne operations.[SRC-16]

### 29.1 Operational implications

- PLAAF may assume greater responsibility for land-based aviation supporting maritime campaigns.
- PLAN aviation remains essential for fleet air defense, carrier power projection, maritime surveillance, and shipborne helicopter or unmanned operations.
- Joint command and service integration become more important because maritime aviation effects cross organizational boundaries.

## 30. PLA Rocket Force

The PLARF operates the PRC's land-based conventional and nuclear ballistic missile forces and related missile capabilities.[SRC-11]

Its conventional roles may include:

- long-range precision strike;
- attacks on airbases and ports;
- attacks on command and information nodes;
- suppression of air and missile defenses;
- attacks on logistics and reinforcement infrastructure;
- maritime strike;
- coercive signaling;
- creation of operational windows for other forces.

### 30.1 Rocket Force advantages

- long range;
- rapid time to target;
- ability to mass effects;
- reduced exposure of aircrews;
- capacity to attack heavily defended fixed facilities;
- mobile launchers that complicate counterforce targeting.

### 30.2 Rocket Force dependencies

- target identification and updating;
- space and other ISR;
- communications;
- mission planning;
- damage assessment;
- reloads and stockpiles;
- launcher survivability;
- deconfliction with other forces.

### 30.3 Conventional-nuclear considerations

The PLARF operates both conventional and nuclear forces. An LLM should not infer nuclear employment from the existence of a missile capability. It should, however, recognize risks from:

- ambiguity about payload;
- attacks on dual-capable units;
- attacks on strategic command;
- warning-system interactions;
- pressure on leaders' decision time.

## 31. Aerospace Force

The Aerospace Force is responsible for military space functions after the 2024 dissolution of the Strategic Support Force.[SRC-02][SRC-07]

Space capabilities support:

- intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance;
- communications;
- positioning, navigation, and timing;
- missile warning;
- environmental monitoring;
- targeting;
- command and control.

Space support is a backbone of long-range precision operations. Its disruption could affect several services simultaneously.

## 32. Cyberspace Force

The Cyberspace Force is one of the strategic arms created or clarified through the 2024 reform.[SRC-02][SRC-07]

Potential operational roles include:

- military network operations;
- cyber defense;
- support to cyber effects;
- technical reconnaissance;
- protection of critical military information systems;
- integration with joint campaigns.

Open sources do not reveal every command relationship or authority. Avoid assigning a specific cyber effect to a specific organization unless the source or game explicitly does so.

## 33. Information Support Force

The Information Support Force (ISF) was established in April 2024 and is directly commanded by the CMC.[SRC-07]

CASI's initial assessment links the ISF to:

- network information systems;
- communications support;
- data and information flow;
- information protection;
- integration of joint operational systems;
- provision of a shared information foundation.

Xi Jinping's public descriptions emphasized information support as essential to coordinated joint operations and victory in modern warfare.[SRC-07]

### 33.1 Why the ISF matters in a game

If the board includes a C2, communications, data, relay, or information-support enabler, it may represent a disproportionately important node. Its loss could affect:

- targeting;
- joint coordination;
- shared awareness;
- command reach;
- tempo;
- cross-domain integration.

## 34. Joint Logistics Support Force

The Joint Logistics Support Force (JLSF) supports joint operations through theater-oriented logistics.[SRC-02]

Functions may include:

- supply;
- transportation;
- medical support;
- storage;
- repair coordination;
- fuel;
- infrastructure support;
- regional and cross-service logistics.

The JLSF does not eliminate service logistics. Joint and service support systems must coordinate.

## 35. Political work and organizational control

Political work seeks to preserve:

- Party control;
- discipline;
- cohesion;
- ideological reliability;
- morale;
- security;
- public and internal narratives.

In a wargame, political work may be represented indirectly through command resilience, willingness to continue, information effects, or scenario objectives. Do not invent morale rules unless present.

## 36. Civil and commercial support

CASI's research mission explicitly includes civilian and commercial infrastructure supporting aerospace forces.[SRC-01]

Relevant support may include:

- airports;
- ports;
- rail and road networks;
- commercial satellite and communications services;
- electronics and manufacturing;
- energy;
- shipping;
- scientific institutions;
- repair capacity;
- data infrastructure.

A system analysis should identify which apparently civilian nodes have military enabling functions, while also recognizing that real-world targeting is governed by law and civilian-protection obligations.

# Part IV — Aerospace and Joint Mission Areas

## 37. Reconnaissance, surveillance, and early warning

An integrated PLA campaign depends on locating, identifying, tracking, and characterizing targets.

Sources may include:

- satellites;
- over-the-horizon radar;
- ground radar;
- airborne early-warning aircraft;
- maritime patrol aircraft;
- unmanned systems;
- electronic intelligence;
- cyber reconnaissance;
- ships and submarines;
- civilian or commercial information.

### 37.1 The reconnaissance-strike relationship

Long-range weapons are useful only if targeting information is:

- accurate;
- timely;
- precise enough;
- communicated to the shooter;
- updated as targets move;
- resilient to deception;
- supported by damage assessment.

### 37.2 Wargame questions

- Which enemy targets are fixed, relocatable, or mobile?
- What sensor supports each attack?
- What happens if the sensor is destroyed or deceived?
- Is the target track current enough?
- Can a different sensor-shooter path be used?
- Does the attack reveal the reconnaissance system?

## 38. Counterair and local air superiority

PLA aerospace operations may seek the degree of air control needed for a specific campaign rather than permanent control everywhere.

Methods can include:

- fighter operations;
- long-range missile attacks on airbases;
- attacks on command and early-warning nodes;
- suppression of air defenses;
- electronic attack;
- cyber effects;
- deception;
- attacks on tankers and support aircraft;
- runway denial;
- integrated surface-to-air defense.

### 38.1 Local and temporary control

A campaign may require a time-limited air-control window over:

- a landing area;
- a strait;
- a missile-launch corridor;
- a naval formation;
- an air assault route;
- a key base;
- a logistics movement.

## 39. Offensive air operations

PLAAF offensive operations may involve:

- fighter sweeps;
- escort;
- interdiction;
- precision strike;
- bomber operations;
- maritime attack;
- attacks on airbases;
- electronic support and attack;
- unmanned operations.

These operations should be understood as part of a joint campaign. Aircraft may exploit conditions created by missiles, cyber activity, electronic attack, deception, or space support.

## 40. Suppression of enemy air defenses

CASI's 2026 study of Chinese SEAD doctrine assesses a conceptually developed approach emphasizing:[SRC-09]

- preplanned effects;
- long-range fires;
- electromagnetic dominance;
- unmanned systems;
- attacks on the air-defense system as a whole;
- engineered windows of air superiority;
- minimizing exposure of valuable crewed aircraft.

### 40.1 System approach to SEAD

Potential targets include:

- search radars;
- fire-control radars;
- launchers;
- command posts;
- communications;
- power;
- logistics;
- interceptor bases;
- data links;
- sensor fusion;
- repair capability.

### 40.2 CASI-assessed limitations

CASI assesses that adaptive, real-time SEAD against a capable and changing integrated air-defense system remains a major challenge. The PLA appears better optimized for:

- prepared target sets;
- scripted joint firepower;
- short opening windows;
- preplanned electromagnetic effects;

than for prolonged, dynamic suppression against a decentralized opponent that relocates, emits selectively, uses decoys, and restores functions.[SRC-09]

This is a relative limitation, not evidence that PLA SEAD is ineffective.

## 41. Integrated air and missile defense

The PRC fields a layered air-defense system combining:

- fighters;
- long-range and shorter-range surface-to-air missiles;
- radars;
- command networks;
- passive detection;
- electronic warfare;
- point defense;
- camouflage, concealment, and deception.

The system protects:

- leadership;
- population and industry;
- bases;
- missile forces;
- ports;
- command centers;
- critical infrastructure;
- maneuvering forces.

### 41.1 Defense as a system

An attacker must consider:

- overlapping coverage;
- sensor diversity;
- mobility;
- emission control;
- decoys;
- fighter integration;
- reloads;
- communications;
- geographic depth;
- recovery.

## 42. Joint firepower strike

A joint firepower strike seeks coordinated effects from several services and domains against critical targets.

A possible strike architecture can include:

- cyber or electronic preparation;
- missile salvos;
- air-delivered weapons;
- naval fires;
- unmanned decoys;
- attacks on warning and command;
- follow-on assessment;
- re-strike.

The purpose may be to:

- paralyze command;
- suppress defenses;
- close bases;
- delay reinforcement;
- isolate a theater;
- coerce political decision;
- support a blockade or landing.

## 43. Airbase attack

Airbases are operational systems, not merely runway coordinates. Their functions include:

- sortie generation;
- command;
- fuel;
- munitions;
- maintenance;
- parking and shelter;
- intelligence;
- air defense;
- personnel support;
- repair.

A strike may seek:

- aircraft destruction;
- temporary runway closure;
- fuel or munitions loss;
- delayed sortie generation;
- forced dispersal;
- consumption of repair resources;
- diversion of air defense;
- psychological or political effect.

### 43.1 Temporary versus durable effect

Runway damage may be repaired. A strong analysis asks:

- How long is the base unavailable?
- Are alternate runways present?
- Are aircraft already dispersed?
- Can fuel and weapons still move?
- Is repair capacity attacked or merely delayed?
- What follow-on action exploits the closure?

## 44. Maritime strike and sea control or denial

PLA aerospace forces support maritime operations through:

- anti-ship missiles;
- bombers and strike aircraft;
- reconnaissance;
- carrier and shipborne aviation;
- electronic warfare;
- air defense;
- mining support;
- attacks on ports and logistics;
- long-range rocket and missile fire.

### 44.1 Anti-ship kill chain

A maritime strike requires:

1. detection;
2. classification;
3. identification;
4. tracking;
5. targeting solution;
6. transmission;
7. weapon employment;
8. midcourse support if required;
9. terminal discrimination;
10. assessment.

Mobile maritime targets place heavy demands on information timeliness and resilience.

## 45. Blockade and isolation

A blockade or isolation campaign may seek to:

- control maritime and air access;
- pressure political leadership;
- interrupt trade or reinforcement;
- demonstrate control without immediate invasion;
- create inspection or exclusion mechanisms;
- shape conditions for later operations.

It can involve:

- naval forces;
- air patrols;
- missiles;
- mines;
- coast guard or maritime law-enforcement forces;
- information and legal claims;
- cyber or economic pressure.

### 45.1 Operational tradeoffs

A blockade can be less immediately destructive than a major strike but may:

- take longer;
- create opportunities for outside intervention;
- require sustained presence;
- expose forces to attrition;
- test political cohesion;
- escalate through enforcement incidents.

## 46. Anti-intervention and counter-intervention

A major PLA campaign problem is preventing or delaying outside forces from influencing a conflict.

Potential methods include:

- attacks on regional bases;
- attacks on ports and logistics;
- long-range maritime strike;
- attacks on tankers and airborne early-warning aircraft;
- cyber and electromagnetic disruption;
- space-related effects;
- submarine operations;
- air and missile defense;
- political pressure on host nations;
- creating multiple simultaneous threats.

The operational objective may be to create enough time and distance to achieve a local objective before reinforcement arrives.

## 47. Space operations and counterspace considerations

Space systems provide:

- wide-area sensing;
- communications;
- navigation;
- timing;
- missile warning;
- weather;
- targeting.

A system-confrontation approach may seek to disrupt these services by physical or nonphysical means.

### 47.1 Caution

Do not assume that every space disruption is permanent or attributable. Analyze:

- reversible versus irreversible effects;
- local versus global impact;
- redundancy;
- commercial substitutes;
- escalation;
- debris and long-term consequences;
- dependence of both sides on the same environment.

## 48. Cyberspace operations

Cyber activity can support:

- intelligence collection;
- preparation of the operational environment;
- disruption;
- deception;
- defense of friendly networks;
- attacks on logistics or command systems;
- support to information operations.

Cyber effects are often:

- access dependent;
- uncertain;
- temporary;
- difficult to assess;
- vulnerable to remediation;
- politically sensitive.

Do not represent cyber as guaranteed paralysis unless the game rules provide that effect.

## 49. Electromagnetic operations

Electromagnetic activity can:

- detect emitters;
- locate units;
- jam radar or communications;
- deceive sensors;
- protect friendly systems;
- support targeting;
- suppress air defenses;
- interfere with navigation;
- shape signatures.

A PLA campaign may seek electromagnetic advantage before exposing high-value platforms.

### 49.1 Emission dilemmas

An air-defense force that emits may reveal its location. A force that remains silent may preserve survivability but reduce awareness and engagement opportunity.

## 50. Information and cognitive operations

CASI-hosted PLA texts on joint campaign information operations describe information activity as an integrated campaign function, not only public messaging.[SRC-12]

Relevant categories may include:

- public opinion activity;
- psychological activity;
- legal argument;
- deception;
- operational security;
- cyber and electromagnetic action;
- attacks on decisionmaking;
- protection of friendly cognition and morale.

### 50.1 Analytical discipline

Do not use “information warfare” as a catch-all. Specify:

- target audience or system;
- desired belief or behavior;
- delivery channel;
- connection to physical operations;
- measure of effect;
- adversary counter-message.

## 51. Unmanned systems and human-machine teaming

PLA aerospace forces are developing and experimenting with:

- reconnaissance UAVs;
- strike UAVs;
- decoys;
- electronic-warfare UAVs;
- loyal-wingman concepts;
- swarms;
- crewed-uncrewed teaming;
- autonomous decision support.

CASI reported evidence that a PLAAF aviation brigade was validating manned-unmanned teaming combat methods by 2024, indicating meaningful progress while leaving operational maturity uncertain.[SRC-18]

### 51.1 Potential operational value

Unmanned systems may:

- increase sensor coverage;
- absorb risk;
- stimulate air defenses;
- create decoys;
- extend communications;
- perform electronic attack;
- complicate tracking;
- add firing pathways.

### 51.2 Limitations

- communications dependence;
- autonomy reliability;
- electromagnetic vulnerability;
- data overload;
- command authority;
- integration with crewed formations;
- uncertain performance in a denied environment.

## 52. Airlift, airborne, and power projection

The PLAAF operates transport aircraft and airborne forces supporting:

- rapid movement;
- reinforcement;
- air assault;
- logistics;
- evacuation;
- strategic signaling;
- operations beyond established ground routes.

Large transports are valuable but vulnerable and require:

- air superiority or protected corridors;
- usable airfields or drop zones;
- planning;
- loading capacity;
- fuel;
- escorts;
- onward support.

## 53. Tactical aviation logistics

CASI's translation of a PLA air-force tactical logistics text shows an aviation support system concerned with:[SRC-14]

- fuel;
- ammunition;
- maintenance and repair;
- transport and loading;
- medical support;
- emergency airfield repair;
- concealment and evacuation of materiel;
- coordination with joint logistics;
- coordination with local governments and civilian resources;
- support relationships among stations and neighboring forces.

### 53.1 Logistics is a combat function

Aviation combat power depends on:

- sortie generation;
- weapons preparation;
- fuel distribution;
- spare parts;
- maintenance;
- runway serviceability;
- personnel;
- ground equipment;
- protection;
- reconstitution.

## 54. Remote and dispersed basing

CASI research on remote PLA logistics highlights the importance of infrastructure, pre-positioning, transportation, local support, and sustainment at austere locations.[SRC-15]

Dispersal can:

- complicate targeting;
- preserve aircraft;
- create more launch directions;
- improve regional coverage;
- reduce dependence on one base.

It also creates:

- supply burdens;
- smaller maintenance capacity;
- communication challenges;
- security requirements;
- uneven runway quality;
- dependence on transport;
- slower repair.

## 55. Nuclear operations and strategic stability

The PRC's nuclear forces are part of strategic deterrence and are not interchangeable with conventional campaign forces.

For conventional wargame analysis:

- do not recommend nuclear use unless explicitly modeled;
- identify when conventional attacks could threaten nuclear command or forces;
- distinguish conventional coercion from nuclear signaling;
- consider ambiguity created by dual-capable systems;
- recognize that attacks on warning and command can create severe escalation risk.

# Part V — Campaign Logic in an Indo-Pacific Scenario

## 56. Campaigns as political-military systems

A PLA campaign should be understood as an organized sequence connecting:

> **Political objective → strategic posture → campaign objective → system effects → joint actions → assessment → termination**

A list of strikes is not a campaign.

## 57. Possible campaign objectives

Depending on the scenario, a PRC player may seek to:

- compel a political concession;
- prevent formal separation or an unwanted political action;
- establish control of a disputed area;
- isolate an island;
- deny foreign intervention;
- impose a blockade;
- protect mainland or maritime approaches;
- seize a limited objective;
- defeat a regional force;
- create a favorable basis for negotiation;
- demonstrate capability and resolve without full-scale war.

The game rules and scenario determine which objectives are actually relevant.

## 58. Coercive signaling campaign

A coercive campaign might use:

- exercises;
- readiness increases;
- patrols;
- missile deployments;
- air and naval presence;
- exclusion areas;
- selective mobilization;
- information activity;
- limited demonstrations.

### 58.1 Desired effect

The opponent changes behavior because it believes:

- escalation is credible;
- resistance will be costly;
- outside support will arrive too late;
- the PRC can control the next step;
- a concession is preferable.

### 58.2 Risk

Repeated signaling without follow-through can reduce credibility. Excessive pressure can unify the opposition or trigger counter-mobilization.

## 59. Joint firepower strike campaign

A joint firepower strike campaign may aim to:

- paralyze command;
- disable air and maritime response;
- suppress defenses;
- close key bases and ports;
- delay reinforcement;
- produce shock;
- create an exploitation window.

### 59.1 Likely logic

1. collect and update target information;
2. position forces and prepare launch units;
3. conceal intent or create deceptive indicators;
4. disrupt warning and information systems;
5. deliver concentrated missile and air effects;
6. assess damage;
7. re-strike key functions;
8. exploit with other forces;
9. communicate political terms or warnings.

### 59.2 Failure modes

- target dispersal;
- inaccurate battle-damage assessment;
- rapid repair;
- resilient command;
- mobile defenses;
- decoys;
- insufficient follow-on forces;
- munitions depletion;
- coalition intervention.

## 60. Air offensive campaign

An air offensive may seek:

- local air superiority;
- destruction or suppression of air forces;
- attacks on bases;
- support to maritime or ground operations;
- defense of missile and naval forces;
- strategic strike.

It may combine PLAAF aircraft with PLARF missiles, information forces, air defenses, and naval aviation.

## 61. Air-defense campaign

An air-defense campaign protects:

- political leadership;
- population centers;
- industrial areas;
- missile forces;
- ports and bases;
- joint forces;
- command networks.

It may emphasize layered defense, geographic depth, fighter and missile integration, deception, mobility, and passive protection.

## 62. Blockade or quarantine-style campaign

A blockade-oriented campaign may combine military and law-enforcement-like activity to regulate access and apply pressure.

Operational requirements include:

- persistent surveillance;
- air and maritime control;
- enforcement forces;
- escalation rules;
- logistics;
- political messaging;
- ability to counter outside escort or intervention.

## 63. Island landing support

An island landing campaign would require integration of:

- missile and air strikes;
- air and maritime superiority;
- mine and obstacle response;
- amphibious lift;
- airborne or special operations;
- logistics;
- information operations;
- protection of staging areas;
- reinforcement;
- follow-on sustainment.

Aerospace power would seek to isolate the objective, suppress defenses, prevent reinforcement, protect transport, and sustain tempo.

### 63.1 The landing is not the end state

A successful initial landing must be followed by:

- buildup;
- supply;
- command;
- air defense;
- port or airfield use;
- protection from counterattack;
- political consolidation.

## 64. Anti-intervention campaign logic

A PRC player may seek to create a regional fait accompli before outside forces can arrive in strength.

Key targets or pressures may include:

- regional airbases;
- ports;
- tanker tracks;
- logistics hubs;
- carrier or surface groups;
- communications;
- space support;
- host-nation political willingness;
- reinforcement routes.

### 64.1 Dilemma creation

The opponent may be forced to choose among:

- protecting bases;
- defending forward forces;
- preserving tankers;
- escorting logistics;
- maintaining maritime access;
- defending political centers;
- supporting allies.

## 65. A synthesized opening-campaign sequence

The following is an analytical synthesis, not a claim of a fixed real-world PLA plan:

### Phase 0 — Shape and prepare

- political and information preparation;
- intelligence collection;
- force positioning;
- logistics buildup;
- cyber access and electromagnetic mapping;
- exercises or demonstrations;
- deception;
- readiness changes.

### Phase 1 — Blind, disrupt, and constrain

- attacks or effects against warning, communications, networks, and key sensors;
- electromagnetic pressure;
- suppression of critical defenses;
- actions to delay political and military coordination.

### Phase 2 — Concentrated firepower

- missile and air strikes on critical nodes;
- airbase and port disruption;
- attacks on high-value enabling forces;
- maritime strike;
- creation of a local operational window.

### Phase 3 — Exploit

- air and maritime operations;
- blockade enforcement;
- movement of landing or airborne forces;
- seizure or isolation of objectives;
- attacks on reinforcement.

### Phase 4 — Sustain, assess, and control

- re-strike;
- repair and reconstitution;
- defense against counterattack;
- logistics;
- political signaling;
- negotiation or termination efforts.

## 66. Branches and sequels

A strong PRC-style campaign analysis should include branches for:

- intervention earlier than expected;
- failure of the initial strike;
- resilient enemy command;
- loss of information support;
- base damage;
- missile stockpile depletion;
- unexpected escalation;
- coalition entry;
- successful coercion without further combat.

# Part VI — Capability, Training, and Doctrinal Limitations

## 67. Doctrine is not demonstrated proficiency

The most important analytic safeguard is to distinguish:

- what the PLA says modern war requires;
- what it is building;
- what it trains;
- what it has demonstrated;
- what it can sustain under combat conditions.

A doctrinally coherent concept may fail because of training, command, logistics, integration, intelligence, or adversary adaptation.

## 68. Limited modern combat experience

The PLA has little recent experience in large-scale, high-technology joint war. Exercises, simulations, competitions, and experimentation can improve readiness but cannot reproduce every feature of combat.

Potential unknowns include:

- performance under casualties;
- adaptation after plans fail;
- joint trust;
- command initiative;
- repair under fire;
- real-time targeting;
- endurance;
- assessment accuracy.

## 69. Scripted versus adaptive operations

CASI's SEAD study assesses that PLA concepts may be strongest in carefully prepared, front-loaded operations and less mature in sustained adaptation against a responsive enemy.[SRC-09]

Signs of this tension include:

- emphasis on detailed preplanning;
- reliance on known target sets;
- centralized coordination;
- difficulty retasking under degraded communications;
- limited evidence of dynamic integration at scale.

## 70. Network dependence

The same information system that creates integration can create vulnerability.

Potential failure points include:

- satellite loss;
- communications disruption;
- data corruption;
- cyber compromise;
- electromagnetic interference;
- loss of relays;
- incompatible systems;
- decision overload;
- inaccurate common operating pictures.

A resilient opponent may seek to fragment the PLA system into isolated units.

## 71. Centralization and initiative

Centralized Party and CMC control can:

- align operations with political objectives;
- prevent unauthorized escalation;
- concentrate national resources;
- improve unity.

It can also:

- slow time-sensitive decisions;
- discourage subordinate initiative;
- amplify the effect of command disruption;
- create dependence on preapproved plans;
- make bad information at the top especially consequential.

## 72. Joint integration

Theater commands and strategic arms are designed to improve joint operations, but jointness must be trained and sustained.

Potential problems include:

- service interests;
- command-boundary ambiguity;
- data interoperability;
- differences in procedure;
- coordination of missile, air, naval, cyber, and space effects;
- battle-damage assessment;
- fratricide and deconfliction;
- logistics responsibility.

## 73. Targeting and assessment

System-destruction warfare depends on correctly identifying critical nodes. Errors can result from:

- outdated intelligence;
- decoys;
- hidden redundancy;
- misunderstanding system relationships;
- optimistic damage assessment;
- rapid repair;
- mobile targets;
- civilian and commercial substitutes.

## 74. Kill-web maturity

CASI's kill-process study warns against treating research papers and diagrams as evidence of a fielded, adaptive kill web.[SRC-08]

A game analysis should distinguish:

- a fixed sensor-to-shooter path;
- a network with alternate paths;
- an intelligent and dynamically reconfiguring mesh.

Do not grant the PRC perfect targeting merely because the doctrine values networks.

## 75. SEAD adaptability

CASI assesses a gap between sophisticated PLA SEAD concepts and demonstrated ability to conduct sustained, dynamic suppression against an adaptive integrated defense.[SRC-09]

An opponent can exploit this with:

- mobility;
- emission control;
- decoys;
- decentralized command;
- rapid restoration;
- unpredictable tactics;
- alternate sensors;
- layered passive defense.

## 76. Hardware and operational adaptation

CASI's study of Chinese perceptions of stealth finds a strong focus on technical and hardware responses, with potential underestimation of the operational adaptability that makes low-observable forces effective.[SRC-10]

The broader lesson is that platform specifications do not equal operational effect. Tactics, support, training, intelligence, and adaptation matter.

## 77. Logistics and endurance

Opening salvos and short operations can conceal sustainment problems. A longer campaign tests:

- missile inventories;
- aircraft maintenance;
- fuel;
- spare parts;
- base repair;
- transport capacity;
- medical support;
- protected storage;
- replacement crews;
- contested movement.

A game move should be assessed for whether it preserves the ability to operate on later turns.

## 78. Remote basing limitations

Remote or dispersed basing can improve survivability but may reduce:

- sortie rate;
- repair capacity;
- weapons availability;
- communications quality;
- security;
- crew support.

Dispersal is useful only when the force remains operational.

## 79. Intelligence uncertainty and deception

A system-focused force can be manipulated if the opponent:

- presents false critical nodes;
- hides redundancy;
- uses decoys;
- creates misleading emissions;
- disperses before attack;
- allows a noncritical target to appear valuable;
- changes its operational pattern after the first strike.

## 80. Escalation-control uncertainty

The desire to control escalation does not ensure common thresholds or mutual understanding. Risk rises when operations involve:

- strategic warning;
- space systems;
- nuclear and conventional co-location;
- attacks on national command;
- homeland bases;
- ambiguous missiles;
- rapid multi-domain effects.

## 81. Avoid two opposite errors

### Error 1 — “The PLA says it, therefore it can do it”

This confuses doctrine with capability.

### Error 2 — “The PLA has limitations, therefore the concept does not matter”

An imperfectly executed system attack can still create severe effects. Limitations change probability and duration, not necessarily intent or danger.

# Part VII — Doctrine-Informed Game Analysis

## 82. Begin with the political-military objective

Ask:

- What outcome does the PRC side need?
- Is the objective deterrence, compellence, denial, seizure, isolation, or destruction?
- Is the desired result reversible?
- What political condition ends the campaign?
- How much escalation is acceptable?
- Is time helping the PRC or the opponent?

## 83. Construct the operational systems

### PRC system

Identify:

- theater command;
- PLAAF forces;
- PLARF forces;
- naval aviation;
- sensors;
- information-support nodes;
- space support;
- cyber and electromagnetic capabilities;
- bases and launch areas;
- air and missile defense;
- logistics;
- political and information instruments;
- reserves.

### Opposing system

Identify:

- decision and command;
- sensors;
- air defenses;
- aircraft;
- tankers;
- bases;
- ports;
- logistics;
- reinforcement;
- alliances;
- communications;
- repair;
- high-value enabling assets.

## 84. Identify the critical operational tension

A good problem statement exposes competing demands.

Weak:

> “The PRC should attack the enemy base.”

Better:

> “The opposing base enables reinforcement and tanker operations, but attacking it may expand the war geographically and consume missiles needed to suppress defenses near the primary objective.”

## 85. Determine the decisive system function

Ask which enemy function most prevents the PRC objective:

- warning;
- command;
- air defense;
- air sortie generation;
- maritime targeting;
- reinforcement;
- logistics;
- political cohesion;
- repair;
- access.

The decisive function may not be represented by the strongest unit.

## 86. Develop distinct courses of action

### COA A — System-paralysis opening

Concentrate information, electromagnetic, missile, and air effects against command, sensors, bases, and key links to create a short exploitation window.

### COA B — Isolation and blockade

Avoid immediate maximal attack, establish control around the objective, disrupt reinforcement, and apply sustained coercion.

### COA C — Defensive counter-intervention posture

Protect the primary objective, preserve missile and air forces, and hold outside bases, tankers, or maritime forces at risk.

### COA D — Limited coercive demonstration

Use a bounded action designed to communicate capability and resolve while retaining an off-ramp.

### COA E — Dispersed preservation

Disperse aviation and launchers, strengthen the information system, absorb pressure, and prepare a later counterattack.

## 87. Wargame each course of action

For each COA, assess:

1. political message;
2. warning indicators;
3. friendly action;
4. opponent's most likely response;
5. opponent's most dangerous response;
6. PRC counteraction;
7. resulting system condition;
8. sustainment;
9. escalation;
10. termination opportunity.

## 88. Doctrinal evaluation rubric

### Political alignment

Does the move create the required political condition?

### Initiative

Does it force the opponent to react?

### Information advantage

Does it improve PRC awareness or degrade the opponent's?

### System effect

Does it disable a critical function or merely damage a replaceable object?

### Joint integration

Are missile, air, maritime, information, space, and logistics effects connected?

### Concentration

Are effects massed against the main operational problem?

### Local air control

Does the action create the required degree of air freedom at the right place and time?

### Precision and timing

Are targets valid, current, and connected to an exploitation window?

### Operational control

Does the move help control pace, geography, intensity, and duration?

### Protection

Does it preserve command, launchers, bases, sensors, and support forces?

### Sustainment

Can the force continue and re-strike?

### Adaptability

Can the plan survive loss of a node or unexpected enemy action?

### Dilemma creation

Does the opponent face several credible problems that cannot all be solved?

### Assessment

Can the PRC player determine whether the desired function was disabled?

## 89. Common errors in analyzing PRC play

### 89.1 Platform mirroring

Assuming every PRC fighter should fight an opposing fighter rather than support a system-level objective.

### 89.2 Treating missiles as independent artillery

Ignoring their dependence on sensing, command, target updates, assessment, and stockpiles.

### 89.3 Assuming a fixed opening script

PLA theory values planning, but actual choices depend on politics, warning, geography, and opponent posture.

### 89.4 Granting perfect joint integration

Organizational reform and doctrinal aspiration do not guarantee frictionless execution.

### 89.5 Ignoring political signaling

An exercise, patrol, or limited strike may aim at decisionmaking rather than physical destruction.

### 89.6 Equating active defense with passivity

Active defense can support offensive operational action.

### 89.7 Equating system destruction with total destruction

Temporary paralysis may be sufficient if exploited.

### 89.8 Ignoring repair and redundancy

A node matters only if its function cannot be quickly restored or substituted.

### 89.9 Treating “gray zone” as the PLA's own fixed category

CASI finds no single official public ladder and notes Chinese authors' discomfort with the term.

### 89.10 Treating escalation control as reliable

The concept expresses intent, not guaranteed mutual control.

### 89.11 Ignoring logistics after the first turn

Front-loaded firepower may culminate if it cannot be sustained.

### 89.12 Using doctrine to invent legal actions

The game rules remain binding.

# Part VIII — Glossary

**Active defense:** PLA strategic concept combining overall strategic defensiveness with active, and potentially offensive, campaign and tactical action.

**Aerospace Force (ASF):** PLA strategic arm responsible for military space functions after the 2024 organizational reform.

**Air offensive campaign:** Joint or air-force operations intended to gain air advantage and attack enemy aerospace and supporting systems.

**Anti-intervention:** Operations intended to prevent, delay, or raise the cost of outside military involvement in a regional conflict.

**Campaign:** A connected series of joint operations designed to accomplish an operational or strategic objective.

**Central Military Commission (CMC):** The Party's highest military command body.

**Compellence:** Use or threatened use of power to cause an opponent to take, stop, or change an action.

**Counter-intervention:** PLA framing for defeating or constraining intervention once it occurs.

**Cyberspace Force (CSF):** PLA strategic arm associated with military cyberspace functions after the 2024 reform.

**Decisive node:** A node whose disruption creates an outsized effect on an operational system.

**Deterrence:** Use or threatened use of power to prevent an opponent from acting.

**Effective control:** A PLA concept concerned with creating favorable posture and controlling crisis and war in scope, pace, intensity, geography, and termination.

**Information domain:** A functional domain encompassing information, networks, data, cognition, electromagnetic activity, and related command processes; exact PLA usage varies.

**Information power:** The ability to acquire, transmit, process, protect, exploit, or deny information in support of operations.

**Information Support Force (ISF):** CMC-controlled strategic arm established in 2024 to support network information systems, communications, data flow, information protection, and joint integration.

**Informationization:** Transformation of warfare through networked information systems, precision weapons, and integrated command.

**Intelligentization:** Emerging transformation associated with artificial intelligence, autonomy, human-machine teaming, and increasingly adaptive operational systems.

**Integrated air and missile defense:** Coordinated use of fighters, surface-to-air missiles, sensors, command systems, electronic warfare, and passive measures to defend airspace and critical assets.

**Integrated strategic deterrence:** Use of military and nonmilitary instruments, capability, resolve, and communication to influence an opponent's decisions.

**Joint firepower strike:** Coordinated application of missile, air, naval, land, cyber, electromagnetic, or other effects against campaign targets.

**Joint Logistics Support Force (JLSF):** PLA strategic arm responsible for major joint and theater logistics functions.

**Kill chain:** A relatively linear sequence connecting sensing, decision, and engagement.

**Kill mesh:** An aspirational, intelligent, adaptive, and reconfigurable sensor-decision-effector architecture.

**Kill web:** A network with multiple possible pathways among sensors, decision nodes, and effectors.

**Local war:** A geographically bounded war that may nevertheless be intense, joint, technologically advanced, and strategically consequential.

**Multidomain integrated joint operations:** PLA concept for unified operations across services, strategic arms, physical domains, and the information domain.

**Multidomain precision warfare:** Emerging PLA concept emphasizing precise cross-domain effects against critical system nodes through integrated networks.

**Operational system:** The connected organization of command, information, sensors, weapons, maneuver, protection, and support that produces combat power.

**People's war:** Strategic tradition connecting military action with broad political, social, economic, industrial, technological, and popular mobilization.

**PLA Air Force (PLAAF):** PLA service responsible for major air operations, air defense, strategic air capabilities, airborne forces, and supporting systems.

**PLA Rocket Force (PLARF):** PLA service responsible for land-based conventional and nuclear missile forces.

**Political work:** Activities that preserve Party leadership, political reliability, discipline, cohesion, morale, and ideological control within the force.

**Strategic arm:** Current CASI term for the Aerospace, Cyberspace, Information Support, and Joint Logistics Support Forces, distinct from the four services.

**Strategic initiative:** Ability to shape the sequence, timing, and conditions of confrontation so the opponent reacts from disadvantage.

**System destruction warfare:** Operational approach seeking to disrupt, paralyze, or destroy critical functions of an opposing operational system while protecting one's own.

**Systems confrontation:** Warfare understood as competition between integrated operational systems rather than isolated platforms.

**Theater command:** Joint operational command responsible for a strategic direction.

**Three Warfares:** Common analytic label for public opinion, psychological, and legal warfare concepts associated with PLA political and information work; application and organizational ownership vary.

**War control:** Effort to regulate the initiation, scale, intensity, pace, geography, and termination of conflict.
